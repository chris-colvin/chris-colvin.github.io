From official values to working prices

Official values are not necessarily the prices goods fetched in 1822. We reprice butter, wheat, oats, sugar and two tea categories using six price proxies. Other valuations are retained on the model’s sterling basis; these account for 77.8% of the original official value of native exports and imports.

Re-exports are estimated at 5.577% of native exports, using the ratio in the 1807–11 returns. Both the remaining official valuations and that earlier ratio introduce uncertainty.

Why linen matters

Ireland exported 46.46 million yards of plain linen. A price change of one old penny per yard (£0.00417) changes native-export earnings by about £194,000, before the re-export adjustment. An export-average linen price therefore matters greatly to the trade balance.

03 / Public flows

Tax and spending

The return records £4.663m in revenue and £4.781m in spending, including debt charges: a net receipt of £0.118m if every payment reached Irish residents. The public-allocation scenarios test how this changes when some spending reached British recipients.

Revenue, expenditure and allocation assumptions

Return No. 301 records £4.663m of ordinary revenue, after merchant repayments but before collection costs. We count collection costs and other public expenditure separately, then ask how much reached recipients within Ireland.

The verified non-loan, non-debt expenditure is £3.666m, leaving an accounting excess of £0.997m before debt charges. Debt interest and management recorded in Ireland add £1.116m.

Counting all those recorded payments locally gives expenditure and debt charges £118,490 above ordinary revenue. This is an accounting-location benchmark, not an observed cross-border transfer.

Why the Treasury’s reported deficiency is a different number

The return reports expenditure of £5,165,157, receipts of £4,847,127 and a deficiency of £318,030. Those totals include loan advances, repayments, imprest and other monies, and a bill adjustment. Borrowing and repayment of loan principal change financial assets and liabilities, so they belong to financing. Interest is a current income payment and is included in our debt-charge category, alongside management costs. The return also excludes sums for national debt reduction.

Public debt: consolidation and Ireland’s burden

1801 · Separate debts, shared expenses.
The Union initially retained separate pre-Union debts. Ireland’s two-seventeenths contribution concerned common expenditure; it was not a permanent allocation of the entire UK debt.

1817 · Consolidation.
The Exchequers and debts were consolidated, pooling liabilities that included Revolutionary and Napoleonic war borrowing. Ireland had already accumulated its own wartime debt. Consolidation ended the fixed contribution system. By our 1822 snapshot, the former Irish debt payable in Britain was already part of the UK debt. The return published in 1823 confirms that no separate account was kept for that portion.

Joseph Hume attributed an annual charge of £2.781m to debt payable in England, associated with a principal of £83.945m. A later statement by Spring Rice identifies that principal as debt transferred in 1817. We have not verified Hume’s underlying annual-charge calculation.

Our treatment: Irish revenue is already counted as an outflow in the fiscal calculation. Deducting an additional historical debt allocation would count a use of that revenue again. Instead, the missing information is interest actually received by Irish residents, wherever paid.

A payment in Dublin does not prove an Irish-resident creditor; a payment in London does not prove a British-resident creditor. Consolidation concerns who owed the debt; our calculation concerns who received current payments. Repayment of principal remains financing.

How to read the result and what remains uncertain

Public and private payments

The first result offsets absentee income against seasonal earnings and the net public flow. The second deducts the merchandise surplus to show how much remains unexplained by the components counted.

The £2m and £4m absentee estimates are historically sourced, but their coverage is not fully specified. Pensions or other public payments might overlap with the fiscal account. We therefore use them as alternative proxies for the private flow, rather than add a separate rent estimate.

Trade and the remaining account

Most trade values still rely on official valuations. Missing shipping, insurance, other income and transfers could change the residual. British earnings on Irish trade cannot be deducted again if already included in import valuations.

A negative residual is a shortfall among the reconstructed flows. It is not by itself evidence of capital flight or a cash shipment. A complete account would also identify financing and statistical discrepancies.

How this relates to Ó Gráda

Ó Gráda identifies missing current-account and debt-transfer data as a gap in Ireland’s monetary history (p. 51). Our reconstruction addresses part of that gap; it does not measure the economic cost of the Union.

Residence, spending and investment

Drawing on the 1835 Poor Inquiry, he reports that nearly two-thirds of identified absentees lived elsewhere within Ireland. That is not a deduction to apply to our estimates of flows to Great Britain.

He stresses how landlords used their income, not simply where they lived. Yet he also questions McCulloch’s defence of absenteeism for assuming full employment (pp. 124–126).

What our figures can establish

Ó Gráda judges the Union’s short-run impact to have been minor, noting that increased military activity partly offset gentry absenteeism in Dublin for a decade (pp. 45–46). This supports counting public expenditure alongside outward payments.

Our narrower finding is a partial outward balance under stated assumptions. A single snapshot cannot establish how much the Union changed income or employment.

His earlier work on rents

Ó Gráda’s “Agricultural Head Rents, Pre-Famine and Post-Famine” (1974) estimates total head rents at about £12m in 1845 (pp. 388–389). He corrects earlier estimates for unlet land and non-farm property, and distinguishes head rents from subletting payments.

These are rents across Ireland, not payments to landlords in Great Britain. The article explicitly cautions against estimating the national rental for the early nineteenth century from the evidence then available (p. 385). It therefore supplies context, rather than a new 1822 absentee estimate or a national growth rate for adjusting Pitt. Our £1.5m remains an illustrative extrapolation using Armagh rents.

Source: Cormac Ó Gráda (1994), Ireland: A New Economic History 1780–1939, chapter 3, pp. 45–46 and 51; chapter 5, pp. 124–126. The £2m–£4m claims come from Historical Notices, not Ó Gráda.

How this relates to the World Balance of Payments project

Nievas and Piketty’s World Balance of Payments project distinguishes merchandise trade, services, investment income and transfers. Its treatment of India highlights how an export surplus could support payments to Britain. We use the same distinction between income outflows and the trade that helps finance them; Ireland’s institutions and evidence require their own reconstruction.

Our fiscal subtotal groups different payment types for this historical exercise. Debt interest is income, while public purchases may include goods or services. The subtotal is not a single modern balance-of-payments category, and recorded expenditure does not measure its benefit to Irish people.

05 / A British comparison

What Britain tells us about Ireland

External accounts · 1816–20 annual average

Britain’s service and investment earnings supported a trade deficit. In our Irish reconstruction, a trade surplus helps finance income paid to owners in Great Britain. The contrast directs attention to ownership: who received the income generated by economic integration?

Great Britain · annual average, 1816–20British £ millionPer person*

Was Ireland a colony?

Our estimates identify an extractive dimension through absentee property income. The public balance is less certain, depending on who received government payments. This contributes to the debate over whether Ireland was a colony; the accounts alone cannot establish how inclusive the relationship was. Britain’s surplus supplies context—the estimated Irish payments are our evidence of extraction.

Extraction, inclusion and colonial status

Here, extraction means income accruing to recipients outside Ireland. A net outward payment alone does not establish coercion, an economic loss of the same amount, or the effect of the Union. A colonial interpretation also requires evidence about the origins and enforcement of ownership and the distribution of political power. Equally, military expenditure and debt payments cannot automatically be treated as benefits shared by Irish residents.

For the wider debate, see Terrence McDonough (ed.), Was Ireland a Colony? Economics, Politics and Culture in Nineteenth-Century Ireland (Irish Academic Press, 2005). Our reconstruction contributes evidence on income flows to that debate; it does not resolve the question of colonial status on its own.

Detailed source notes

Absentee estimates: provenance and coverage

Pitt’s cautious benchmark. In his speech of 3 December 1798, Pitt discussed British residents receiving income from Irish property. He admitted having “no accurate data”: £1m was an older benchmark, already exceeded as rents rose. His following calculation nevertheless retained an implied £1m allowance for Ireland within £5m of overseas income, alongside £4m from the West Indies. We have checked the original printed speech, second edition (1808), vol. II, pp. 441–442. Cuenca Esteban (2001), p. 59, note 8, led us to this passage.

How Pitt’s estimate is adjusted. Solar and Hens (2013), Table 3, p. 56, estimate County Armagh rent per acre at £0.69 in 1795–99 and £1.02 in 1820–24 using their regression method. Their ratio gives £1m × 1.02 / 0.69 = £1.478m, rounded to £1.5m in the scenario. This treats Pitt’s cautious allowance as a late-1790s starting point and applies Armagh new-lease rent growth, holding ownership and recipient residence fixed. The original £1m benchmark has no precise date, and existing estate receipts need not follow new rents. The result is an illustrative lower scenario, not a measured national income series.

Historical Notices (Dublin, 1822), p. 61, quotes The Absentee (1820) as estimating £2m sterling spent outside Ireland. Page 62 attributes “near” £4m to Parker’s Plea for the Poor and Industrious, without stating the currency there. Page 115 repeats both as annual income derived from Ireland and spent abroad. This repetition clarifies the quoting author’s interpretation; it is not independent corroboration.

The original pamphlets have been identified but not inspected: The Absentee; or, A Brief Examination into the Habits and Condition of the People of Ireland, by an officer of the customs, London: Rodwell & Martin, 1820, iv + 42 pages (Goldsmiths’–Kress entry 22984, reel 2241; catalogue attribution to [Symes]); and W. Parker, A Plea for the Poor and Industrious, Cork: J. Bolster, 1819, xxvi + 159 + [6] pages (OCLC 62367685). Goldsmiths’–Kress catalogue · Parker catalogue record.

The £2m claim has explicit sterling provenance, not necessarily greater accuracy. We round the upper claim to £4m and assume sterling. Neither quotation itemises pensions, salaries, mortgage interest, temporary travel, estate expenses or debts. Possible overlap with public payments remains unresolved; no numerical deduction is justified by the evidence presently available.

Spending abroad is not interchangeable with gross rent or property income payable to nonresidents. A resident’s foreign travel is a service import; income accruing to an overseas owner is property income. Remittance and accrual can differ, and the same underlying flow must be counted only once. Nor should these explicitly overseas claims be reduced by the domestic-absentee proportion reported for 1835.

Public accounts: reconciliation and debt

HC 1823 (301), Revenue and Expenditure (Ireland), covers the year ending 5 January 1823. The Treasury signed it on 28 April; it was ordered printed on 29 April. The supplied scan, pp. 2–3, was checked against the workbook. Amounts are individually rounded to whole pounds. The return does not explicitly label its pounds sterling; our sterling interpretation follows Hume’s explicitly sterling total.

Ordinary revenue after merchant repayments is £4,662,933. Collection costs (£883,140) and other pre-Exchequer payments (£273,013) are distinct rows, without overlap. Total non-loan, non-debt expenditure is £3,665,515, leaving £997,418 before recorded debt interest and management of £1,115,908.

The full return also includes public works advances (£383,734), explicit repayments (£81,516), imprest and other monies (£79,876), and a bill adjustment (£22,801). Imprest monies are not automatically loan repayments. Reported expenditure of £5,165,157 less receipts of £4,847,127 gives the reported £318,030 deficiency. Individually rounded receipt components are £1 lower; no balancing entry has been invented. The return excludes £645,381 for national debt reduction, also excluded here.

The allocation percentages are sensitivity assumptions, not measured residence shares. Collection location need not establish tax incidence; payment location need not establish creditor residence. Government purchases may overlap merchandise imports, while interest received in Britain by Irish residents remains unmeasured.

Hume’s speech of 25 June 1823 (vol. 9, cols. 1218–19) attributes £2,780,791 annually to £83,944,904 of debt payable in England. Spring Rice’s speech of 23 April 1834 (cols. 1231–33) identifies that principal as debt transferred in 1817, but does not verify Hume’s annual charge. Return 301 says the Britain-payable debt had been consolidated and no separate account was kept. Hume’s roughly £3.099m burden combines that attributed charge with the return’s deficiency; it is not an additional observed cross-border payment to deduct from revenue already counted.
