02 / The public accounts

Revenue and expenditure

Return No. 301 records £4.663m of ordinary revenue, after merchant repayments but before collection costs. We count collection costs and other public expenditure separately, then ask how much reached recipients within Ireland.

The verified non-loan, non-debt expenditure is £3.666m, leaving an accounting excess of £0.997m before debt charges. Debt interest and management recorded in Ireland add £1.116m.

Counting all those recorded payments locally gives expenditure and debt charges £118,490 above ordinary revenue. This is an accounting-location benchmark, not an observed cross-border transfer.

The collection-cost headings in the original return are distinct; we checked that they do not overlap. Whole-pound rounding produces a £1 discrepancy in the return’s receipt reconciliation. No balancing entry has been invented.

Why the Treasury’s reported deficiency is a different number

The return reports expenditure of £5,165,157, receipts of £4,847,127 and a deficiency of £318,030. Those totals include loan advances, repayments, imprest and other monies, and a bill adjustment. Loan principal belongs to financing, so our ordinary fiscal calculation does not treat it as income or consumption expenditure. The return also excludes sums for national debt reduction.

03 / Sovereign debt

How public debt enters the calculation

1801 · Separate debts, shared expenses.
The Union initially retained separate pre-Union debts. Ireland’s two-seventeenths contribution concerned common expenditure; it was not a permanent allocation of the entire UK debt.

1817 · Consolidation.
The Exchequers and debts were consolidated. The fixed contribution system ended. Later attempts to attribute an “Irish” debt burden must be distinguished from actual payments to creditors.

1823 · The return’s explicit limitation.
For the Britain-payable part of the former Irish debt, the Treasury stated that it had been consolidated and no separate account was kept.

Joseph Hume attributed an annual charge of £2.781m to debt payable in England, associated with a principal of £83.945m. A later statement by Spring Rice identifies that principal as debt transferred in 1817. We have not verified Hume’s underlying annual-charge calculation.

Our treatment: Irish revenue is already counted as an outflow in the fiscal calculation. Deducting an additional historical debt allocation would count a use of that revenue again. Instead, the missing information is interest actually received by Irish residents, wherever paid.

A payment in Dublin does not prove an Irish-resident creditor; a payment in London does not prove a British-resident creditor. Debt principal and loan repayments must also be separated from interest.

04 / Reading the result

A useful reconstruction, with visible limits.

What the absentee range means

The 1822 Historical Notices quotes £2m sterling from The Absentee (1820) and near £4m from Parker’s Plea for the Poor and Industrious (1819). It describes spending outside Ireland. £3m is our chosen midpoint, not Ó Gráda’s estimate.

The quotations do not settle whether pensions, salaries, mortgage interest or temporary travel are included. We have made no invented adjustment for these items. Potential overlap with the fiscal calculation remains unresolved.

Ó Gráda’s discussion of domestic absenteeism does not justify cutting these explicitly overseas claims by the domestic-absentee share. Nor can spending abroad automatically be equated with gross landlord rents.

What could change the balance

About 77.8% of the original official value of native exports and imports remains outside our six commodity price proxies. Carrying official values forward is a major valuation assumption; the trade surplus is not an observed market-price total.

Other services, income receipts and transfers remain incomplete. Public purchases could overlap recorded merchandise imports, and interest paid in Britain to Irish residents is not measured. These omissions could move the result in either direction.

A minus sign identifies an unexplained outward balance in this partial reconstruction. It does not by itself establish capital flight, a cash shipment, or the financing mechanism. A complete balance-of-payments account would also record the offsetting financial entries and any statistical discrepancy.

Per-person amounts and “new money”

We divide by the 1821 all-island census population of 6,801,827, including children. Twenty shillings equal one pound; twelve old pence equal one shilling. Decimal pounds in parentheses express the same historical sterling amount—not today’s purchasing power. Old pence and decimal pounds are rounded independently.

05 / A British comparison

Britain’s service and investment income

A reconstruction labelled Great Britain for 1816–20 shows why the merchandise account alone is insufficient. Its £4.9m annual goods deficit was more than covered by £8.3m in shipping, insurance and mercantile earnings; net debt-servicing receipts added £2.3m.
