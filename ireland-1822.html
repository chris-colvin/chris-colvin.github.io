01 / Why 1823?

Why the eve of 1823?

Political union in 1801 was followed by gradual economic integration: the Exchequers and debts were consolidated in 1817, with customs changes and currency unification following in the 1820s. This snapshot captures Ireland near the end of that transition, while the surviving trade and fiscal returns still allow us to assemble the main components. Gaps in the data make a comparable reconstruction much harder after the 1820s. A separate estimate later in the century is planned, when the evidence becomes fuller again.

Our trade and fiscal returns cover the year ending 5 January 1823—essentially 1822. This common accounting period lets us combine trade and public finances with contemporary claims about absentee spending.

Historical context: Ó Gráda, chapter 3, pp.45–46 and 51. Currency unification followed in 1826.

02 / The finding

A net outward balance after public and private flows

Our reconstruction leaves an annual deficit of £1.17m–£3.17m after combining trade, absentee spending and the recorded public accounts. A £0.71m trade surplus and £0.12m net public receipt together cover only part of the assumed £2m–£4m private outflow.

The distribution of property income matters alongside taxation: income derived from Irish assets could be spent abroad even while recorded state payments slightly exceeded Irish revenue. The absentee claims are our provisional measure of that private outflow; their precise composition remains uncertain.

This headline counts all recorded public payments as Irish receipts. Alternative recipient allocations increase the shortfall. It remains a partial external balance: missing flows and uncertain trade prices prevent a definitive total.

The public component of that combined balance

Recorded spending exceeded revenue by £118,490

On the recorded accounts, the UK state ran a small fiscal deficit in Ireland. Ireland was a net recipient, not a net contributor. Ordinary revenue was £4,662,933; ordinary expenditure and recorded debt charges totalled £4,781,423. The difference was £118,490 in Ireland’s favour—about 4d (£0.02) per person.

Before debt charges, revenue exceeded spending by £997,418. Including £1,115,908 in debt interest and management reverses that balance. The recorded fiscal accounts therefore do not show net extraction from Ireland once those charges are included.

This is the recorded accounting balance, excluding loan principal and other financing items. It is not proof of the actual cross-border transfer: some payments recorded in Ireland may have reached nonresidents, and Irish residents may have received interest in Britain. The allocation scenarios below explore that unresolved distinction.

Why linen matters

Ireland exported 46.46 million yards of plain linen. A price change of one old penny per yard (£0.00417) changes native-export earnings by about £194,000, before the re-export adjustment. An export-average linen price therefore matters greatly to the trade balance.

05 / The public accounts

Revenue and expenditure

Return No. 301 records £4.663m of ordinary revenue, after merchant repayments but before collection costs. We count collection costs and other public expenditure separately, then ask how much reached recipients within Ireland.

The verified non-loan, non-debt expenditure is £3.666m, leaving an accounting excess of £0.997m before debt charges. Debt interest and management recorded in Ireland add £1.116m.

Counting all those recorded payments locally gives expenditure and debt charges £118,490 above ordinary revenue. This is an accounting-location benchmark, not an observed cross-border transfer.

Why the Treasury’s reported deficiency is a different number

The return reports expenditure of £5,165,157, receipts of £4,847,127 and a deficiency of £318,030. Those totals include loan advances, repayments, imprest and other monies, and a bill adjustment. Loan principal belongs to financing, so our ordinary fiscal calculation does not treat it as income or consumption expenditure. The return also excludes sums for national debt reduction.

06 / Sovereign debt

How public debt enters the calculation

1801 · Separate debts, shared expenses.
The Union initially retained separate pre-Union debts. Ireland’s two-seventeenths contribution concerned common expenditure; it was not a permanent allocation of the entire UK debt.

1817 · Consolidation.
The Exchequers and debts were consolidated, ending the fixed contribution system. By our 1822 snapshot, the former Irish debt payable in Britain was already part of the UK debt. The return published in 1823 confirms that no separate account was kept for that portion.

Joseph Hume attributed an annual charge of £2.781m to debt payable in England, associated with a principal of £83.945m. A later statement by Spring Rice identifies that principal as debt transferred in 1817. We have not verified Hume’s underlying annual-charge calculation.

Our treatment: Irish revenue is already counted as an outflow in the fiscal calculation. Deducting an additional historical debt allocation would count a use of that revenue again. Instead, the missing information is interest actually received by Irish residents, wherever paid.

A payment in Dublin does not prove an Irish-resident creditor; a payment in London does not prove a British-resident creditor. Debt principal and loan repayments must also be separated from interest.

07 / Reading the result

A useful reconstruction, with visible limits.

What the absentee range means

The 1822 Historical Notices quotes £2m sterling from The Absentee (1820) and near £4m from Parker’s Plea for the Poor and Industrious (1819). It describes spending outside Ireland. £3m is our chosen midpoint, not Ó Gráda’s estimate.

The quotations do not settle whether pensions, salaries, mortgage interest or temporary travel are included. We have made no invented adjustment for these items. Potential overlap with the fiscal calculation remains unresolved.

Ó Gráda’s discussion of domestic absenteeism does not justify cutting these explicitly overseas claims by the domestic-absentee share. Nor can spending abroad automatically be equated with gross landlord rents.

What could change the balance

About 77.8% of the original official value of native exports and imports remains outside our six commodity price proxies. Carrying official values forward is a major valuation assumption; the trade surplus is not an observed market-price total.

Other services, income receipts and transfers remain incomplete. Public purchases could overlap recorded merchandise imports, and interest paid in Britain to Irish residents is not measured. These omissions could move the result in either direction.

A minus sign identifies an unexplained outward balance in this partial reconstruction. It does not by itself establish capital flight, a cash shipment, or the financing mechanism. A complete balance-of-payments account would also record the offsetting financial entries and any statistical discrepancy.

Per-person amounts and “new money”

We divide by the 1821 all-island census population of 6,801,827, including children. Twenty shillings equal one pound; twelve old pence equal one shilling. Decimal pounds in parentheses express the same historical sterling amount—not today’s purchasing power. Old pence and decimal pounds are rounded independently.

08 / Historical interpretation

How this relates to Ó Gráda

Ó Gráda identifies missing current-account and debt-transfer data as a gap in Ireland’s monetary history (p.51). Our reconstruction addresses part of that gap; it does not measure the economic cost of the Union.

Residence, spending and investment

Drawing on the 1835 Poor Inquiry, he reports that nearly two-thirds of identified absentees lived elsewhere within Ireland. That is not a deduction to apply to our separate claims about overseas spending.

He stresses how landlords used their income, not simply where they lived. Yet he also questions McCulloch’s defence of absenteeism for assuming full employment (pp.124–126).

What our figures can establish

Ó Gráda judges the Union’s short-run impact to have been minor, noting that increased military activity partly offset gentry absenteeism in Dublin for a decade (pp.45–46). This supports counting public expenditure alongside outward payments.

Our narrower finding is a partial outward balance under stated assumptions. A single snapshot cannot establish how much the Union changed income or employment.

Source: Cormac Ó Gráda (1994), Ireland: A New Economic History 1780–1939, chapter 3, pp.45–46 and 51; chapter 5, pp.124–126. The £2m–£4m claims come from Historical Notices, not Ó Gráda.

Detailed source notes

Absentee estimates: provenance and coverage

Historical Notices (Dublin, 1822), p.61, quotes The Absentee (1820) as estimating £2m sterling spent outside Ireland. Page 62 attributes “near” £4m to Parker’s Plea for the Poor and Industrious, without stating the currency there. Page 115 repeats both as annual income derived from Ireland and spent abroad. This repetition clarifies the quoting author’s interpretation; it is not independent corroboration.

The original pamphlets have been identified but not inspected: The Absentee; or, A Brief Examination into the Habits and Condition of the People of Ireland, by an officer of the customs, London: Rodwell & Martin, 1820, iv + 42 pages (Goldsmiths’–Kress entry 22984, reel 2241; catalogue attribution to [Symes]); and W. Parker, A Plea for the Poor and Industrious, Cork: J. Bolster, 1819, xxvi + 159 + [6] pages (OCLC 62367685). Goldsmiths’–Kress catalogue · Parker catalogue record.

The £3m midpoint is illustrative. The lower claim has clearer currency provenance, not necessarily greater accuracy. We round the upper claim to £4m and assume sterling. Neither quotation itemises pensions, salaries, mortgage interest, temporary travel, estate expenses or debts. Possible overlap with public payments remains unresolved; no numerical deduction is justified by the evidence presently available.

Spending abroad is not interchangeable with gross rent or property income payable to nonresidents. A resident’s foreign travel is a service import; income accruing to an overseas owner is property income. Remittance and accrual can differ, and the same underlying flow must be counted only once. Nor should these explicitly overseas claims be reduced by the domestic-absentee proportion reported for 1835.

Public accounts: reconciliation and debt

HC 1823 (301), Revenue and Expenditure (Ireland), covers the year ending 5 January 1823. The Treasury signed it on 28 April; it was ordered printed on 29 April. The supplied scan, pp.2–3, was checked against the workbook. Amounts are individually rounded to whole pounds. The return does not explicitly label its pounds sterling; our sterling interpretation follows Hume’s explicitly sterling total.

Ordinary revenue after merchant repayments is £4,662,933. Collection costs (£883,140) and other pre-Exchequer payments (£273,013) are distinct rows, without overlap. Total non-loan, non-debt expenditure is £3,665,515, leaving £997,418 before recorded debt interest and management of £1,115,908.

The full return also includes public works advances (£383,734), explicit repayments (£81,516), imprest and other monies (£79,876), and a bill adjustment (£22,801). Imprest monies are not automatically loan repayments. Reported expenditure of £5,165,157 less receipts of £4,847,127 gives the reported £318,030 deficiency. Individually rounded receipt components are £1 lower; no balancing entry has been invented. The return excludes £645,381 for national debt reduction, also excluded here.

The allocation percentages are sensitivity assumptions, not measured residence shares. Collection location need not establish tax incidence; payment location need not establish creditor residence. Government purchases may overlap merchandise imports, while interest received in Britain by Irish residents remains unmeasured.

Hume’s speech of 25 June 1823 (vol.9, cols.1218–19) attributes £2,780,791 annually to £83,944,904 of debt payable in England. Spring Rice’s speech of 23 April 1834 (cols.1231–33) identifies that principal as debt transferred in 1817, but does not verify Hume’s annual charge. Return 301 says the Britain-payable debt had been consolidated and no separate account was kept. Hume’s roughly £3.099m burden combines that attributed charge with the return’s deficiency; it is not an additional observed cross-border payment to deduct from revenue already counted.

British comparison: definitions and limitations

Patrick Karl O’Brien (2011), The Contributions of Warfare with Revolutionary and Napoleonic France to the Consolidation and Progress of the British Industrial Revolution, LSE Working Paper 150/11, Table X (printed p.71; PDF p.73), labels its reconstruction Great Britain. It draws on J. Cuenca Esteban (2001), “The British balance of payments, 1772–1820: India transfers and war finance”, Economic History Review 54(1), 58–86. The original table and its treatment of Anglo-Irish flows remain to be checked.

For 1816–20, annual averages in millions of pounds are domestic exports 41.5, re-exports 13.5 and imports 59.9, giving merchandise −4.9. Services add 8.3; warfare, India and migrant transfers contribute −0.9; net debt-servicing receipts add 2.3. The table reports a current-account balance of +4.9, although these rounded components imply +4.8. Its printed formula labels also contain sign inconsistencies. We use the component balances for context, rather than rank a complete British account against a partial Irish one.

British imports are valued including cost, insurance and freight, with a conjectural allowance for illegal imports. Coverage and valuation therefore differ from the Irish model. British net investment receipts and Irish absentee spending are also different measures.

Per-person figures use the 1821 census populations: Ireland 6,801,827; Great Britain 14,091,757, excluding the islands in the British seas. This is a common scale benchmark, not Britain’s mean population in 1816–20. Twenty shillings equal £1 and twelve old pence equal one shilling; displayed old pence and decimal pounds are rounded independently from unrounded amounts.

An alternative reconstruction in Davis and Gallman (2001), Evolving Financial Markets and International Capital Flows, chapter 2, reports UK current-account surpluses of £13.3m in 1822 and £10.3m on average in 1821–25. We do not use these as Great Britain figures because their geographic basis has not been established as excluding Ireland.
