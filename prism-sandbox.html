01 / The starting point

What makes economic history applied?

Christopher L. Colvin · Queen’s University Belfast
Johan Fourie · Stellenbosch University

Economic history begins with a historical question.

Applied economic history begins with a present policy question.

  • Use historical research to assess policy options and their consequences.
  • The research may have been undertaken for other purposes.
  • Systematically compare positive and negative features.
  • Relevance has to be argued: a familiar precedent is not enough.

Positive features are similarities; negative features are differences. Neither term means a favourable or unfavourable outcome.

03 / What we compare

Cases, analogies, narratives and scenarios

TermWhat it isRole in an application
CaseA historical episode bounded in time and place.Supplies evidence about events, actors and outcomes.
AnalogyA comparison of relevant similarities and differences.Connects a historical case to the present problem.
NarrativeAn account explaining how events and actions are connected.Explains why the correspondences might matter.
ScenarioA possible future under specified conditions.Asks what might follow under present conditions and policy choices.

These interact: a new interpretation can change the analogy or the cases selected.

Four uses of narrative

UseWhat it doesWhat to investigate
Scholarly explanationConnects evidence into an account of causes and consequences.What supports or challenges the explanation?
Popular storyHelps people interpret economic events and form expectations.Does its circulation affect expectations or behaviour?
Institutional memoryConnects past experience to an organisation’s responsibilities.How is it preserved, revised and used in decisions?
Public framingEmphasises particular problems, responsibilities and responses.What is left out, and how do audiences interpret it?

One account can serve several uses; each requires different evidence.

06 / Professional practice

Training, communication and support

  • Practise judgement: defend advice as new evidence emerges.
  • Communicate clearly: explain assumptions, limits and uncertainty.
  • Build relationships: reach relevant audiences before decisions.
  • Support the work: recognise collaboration alongside publications.

Historical research builds what Tosh calls an “inventory of alternatives” for future policy questions.

Evidence and limits of the exercise

The conditional text is authored teaching material based on the paper; it does not calculate effects, rank policies or assign probabilities. Selecting a case adds a perspective, not evidence that the mechanism necessarily carries over.

Sources below are listed consistently in full, using the paper’s bibliography. Studies published after 2008 support retrospective assessment; they were not available at the start of the crisis.

  • Patrice Baubeau, Eric Monnet, Angelo Riva and Stefano Ungaro (2021). Flight-to-Safety and the Credit Crunch: A New History of the Banking Crises in France during the Great Depression. The Economic History Review 74(1), 223–250. Read source.

    Broadens the evidence on French banking distress beyond a few large banks.

  • Ben S. Bernanke (2013). The Crisis as a Classic Financial Panic. Speech, Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System · 2013-11-08. Read source.

    Develops the comparison between the Panic of 1907 and the Global Financial Crisis.

  • Michael D. Bordo, Angela Redish and Hugh Rockoff (2015). Why Didn't Canada Have a Banking Crisis in 2008 (or in 1930, or 1907, or …)?. The Economic History Review 68(1), 218–243. Read source.

    Examines how banking structure and regulation contributed to Canadian stability.

  • Christina Boswell (2009). The Political Uses of Expert Knowledge: Immigration Policy and Social Research. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.

    Distinguishes informing policy from substantiating preferences and establishing authority.

  • Robert F. Bruner and Sean D. Carr (2007). The Panic of 1907. New York: Wiley.

    Provides historical detail on the Panic of 1907.

  • Victor Degorce and Eric Monnet (2024). The Great Depression as a Savings Glut. The Journal of Economic History 84(3), 874–916. Read source.

    Examines deposit reallocation towards savings institutions and flight to safety across countries.

  • Barry Eichengreen (2021). Gold and South Africa's Great Depression. Economic History of Developing Regions 36(2), 175–193. Read source.

    Explains how gold production shaped South Africa's gains from depreciation.

  • Barry Eichengreen, Asmaa El-Ganainy, Rui Esteves and Kris James Mitchener (2021). In Defense of Public Debt. Oxford: Oxford University Press. Read source.

    Chapter 12 examines fiscal consolidation in relation to monetary and exchange-rate constraints.

  • Robert M. Entman (1993). Framing: Toward Clarification of a Fractured Paradigm. Journal of Communication 43(4), 51–58.

    Explains how framing makes particular problems, responsibilities and responses salient.

  • Philip T. Fliers and Christopher L. Colvin (2022). Going Dutch: Monetary Policy in the Netherlands during the Interwar Gold Standard, 1925–1936. Financial History Review 29(2), 121–151.

    Distinguishes Dutch monetary flexibility within gold from changing the commitment to parity.

  • Kris James Mitchener and Gary Richardson (2019). Network Contagion and Interbank Amplification during the Great Depression. Journal of Political Economy 127(2), 465–507.

    Examines the propagation of distress through interbank connections.

  • Eric Monnet (2021). Why Central Bankers Should Read Economic History. Books & Ideas · 2021-02-08. Read source.

    Questions how policy problems and the purposes of instruments are defined.

  • Ruben Peeters and Amaury de Vicq de Cumptich (2025). A Series of (Un)fortunate Events: Commercial Bank Interest Rates and Deposit Reallocation during the Great Depression in the Netherlands. The Economic History Review. Advance online publication. Read source.

    Emphasises relative interest rates and policy incentives in Dutch deposit reallocation.

  • Christina D. Romer (1990). The Great Crash and the Onset of the Great Depression. The Quarterly Journal of Economics 105(3), 597–624. Read source.

    Examines uncertainty and the postponement of durable consumption.

  • Christina D. Romer (2009). Lessons from the Great Depression for Economic Recovery in 2009. Speech, Brookings Institution, Washington, DC · 2009-03-09. Read source.

    Uses Depression research to assess recovery policy during the Global Financial Crisis.

  • Christie Swanepoel and Philip T. Fliers (2021). The Fuel of Unparalleled Recovery: Monetary Policy in South Africa between 1925 and 1936. Economic History of Developing Regions 36(2), 213–244. Read source.

    Examines the monetary conditions supporting South African recovery.

  • Eugene N. White (1984). A Reinterpretation of the Banking Crisis of 1930. The Journal of Economic History 44(1), 119–138.

    Examines vulnerabilities preceding bank failures in 1930.

08 / Take it back to your research

Try it with your own research

  1. Identify a present policy question and who faces the decision.
  2. Choose a historical case and explain the relevant mechanism.
  3. Identify a difference that could change the outcome.
  4. Set out two scenarios and the evidence that would lead you to revise your advice.

The aim is to make uncertainty specific enough to inform a choice.
