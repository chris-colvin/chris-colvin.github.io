IRELAND · 1845–1852

Death, Migration and the Great Irish Famine

Death, migration and an economy transformed.

A failed crop became a catastrophe. Economic history asks why its effects were so unequal, how people and institutions responded, and what happened to those who survived.

Dr Chris Colvin Queen’s University BelfastNovember 2025

01

Before the Famine

An economy already changing

Ireland entered the 1840s with widespread rural poverty, a growing population and an economy linked to industrialising Britain.

The potato supported a large population on limited land. But dependence on it was only part of the story. Access to capital, agricultural practices, regional industrial development and migration all shaped living standards.

Was Ireland simply overpopulated? Joel Mokyr’s tests challenged that explanation. Later work using more detailed local data finds a more complicated relationship between population pressure and poverty. The scale of the evidence matters: county averages can conceal large differences between communities.

02

The shock

A crop failure that kept returning

The blight was an external shock. Its consequences depended on institutions and policy.

1845

About one-third of the potato crop was lost.

1846

Near-total crop failure. Excess mortality rose.

1847

Yields improved, but the area planted had fallen sharply.

1848

Another crop failure prolonged the crisis.

Approximately one million excess deaths is the familiar estimate. “Excess” means deaths beyond those expected without the Famine, rather than simply all deaths during those years. Disease was the main recorded killer; malnutrition increased vulnerability.

The burden was unequal. Landless labourers had few assets to sell, and the poorest households had the least capacity to withstand repeated harvest failures. Mortality varied sharply across Ireland.

Think about it: why count “excess” deaths?

Some deaths would have occurred even without the Famine. Estimating the additional loss requires a counterfactual, alongside evidence on births, migration and population change. Missing births add another dimension to the demographic shock.

03

Food and relief

Availability, purchasing power and policy

Food could be present without being within reach.

Food exports continued during the Famine. Retaining those exports alone could not replace the lost potato crop. Imports increased, but access to food still depended on income and relief.

Ireland’s 1838 Poor Law adapted the English New Poor Law, with important differences in entitlement and provision. Workhouses and locally financed relief faced extraordinary demands. The lecture examines these arrangements alongside the financial crisis of 1847 and the constraints on government policy.

Who could obtain food, and on what terms?

That question connects markets to institutions. It also helps explain why selling livestock, borrowing, drawing on savings or entering a workhouse could be central to survival.

Read further: The Irish Poor Law · Ó Gráda on the Great Irish Famine

04

Leaving Ireland

Escape had a price

Migration was a response to the crisis, but it was not an option everyone could afford.

Irish emigration long predated the Famine. The catastrophe accelerated it, while the cost of passage excluded many of the most destitute. Family resources and assistance could determine who left and who remained.

This matters for interpreting the evidence. Emigrants, those who died and those who stayed were not interchangeable groups. Comparing their outcomes requires attention to selection: the characteristics that made someone more likely to appear in each group.

Think about it: did the worst-hit counties necessarily send the most emigrants?

Not necessarily. Severe deprivation could increase the desire to leave while reducing the ability to pay for passage. Death and emigration therefore need not follow the same regional pattern.

05

Lives after the Famine

Connections and human capital

The demographic break reshaped more than the size of the population.

The later sections of the lecture follow changes in literacy, language, numeracy and health. Ireland narrowed its economic gap with Britain, although convergence remained incomplete. Migration, capital accumulation and productivity all enter the explanation.

Ireland’s railway network expanded substantially after the Famine, even as its population continued to fall. The maps invite a question: how did better connections shape economic opportunities and the decision to leave?

Research on the language shift emphasises education, household choices and local transmission. Other work uses the tendency to round reported ages to study numeracy, and prison records to investigate the health of survivors.

Scarring

Famine exposure can damage development, leaving survivors shorter as adults.

Selection

If the most vulnerable die, the surviving group can appear healthier. Migration also changes who remains.

These effects can coexist. A population showing little average height loss may still have suffered severe harm. The research discussed in the lecture compares rural Tipperary and urban Dublin to examine that tension.

Think about it: can healthier-looking survivors conceal a worse crisis?

Yes. The people measured after a crisis are those who survived and remained available to observe. An apparently healthier average can reflect who is missing, rather than an absence of damage.

Teaching focus

  • Compare explanations of pre-Famine poverty and population pressure.
  • Connect food access, institutions and relief policy.
  • Distinguish scarring from selection in evidence about survivors.

Teaching note

  • Delivered at Queen’s University Belfast in November 2025 as part of ECO3020 (Economic History), an elective module for third-year undergraduate students in Economics, Philosophy, Politics and Economics (PPE), and related programmes.
  • Designed for a two-hour teaching block.
  • An accompanying tutorial asks students to discuss and debate selected readings from the module’s reading list.

Questions for discussion

  1. What evidence would distinguish a population-pressure explanation from an institutional explanation?
  2. How could the cost of migration shape who survived and who left?
  3. What can prison records reveal, and whom do they leave out?
