# Established Church return, HC 1820 (93): fiscal research notes


Reviewed 14 November 2026. Title: *Papers relating to the State of the Established Church of Ireland*. Ordered printed 26 May 1820. The principal returns describe 1819 conditions; some financial and construction information refers to earlier years.

## Finding for the fiscal reconstruction

This is a survey of ecclesiastical organisation, residence and physical infrastructure, not a complete account of Church revenue or expenditure. It cannot supply a defensible national annual Church-income total around 1822. Earlier identification of this paper as a promising source for that total should be qualified accordingly.

The questionnaire (p. 7) asks about benefices, parishes, unions, clergy, residence, churches, glebe houses and land. Its monetary questions concern inadequately endowed livings and curates' allowances where incumbents are permitted to be non-resident. It does not ask for the annual income of every benefice, total episcopal estate receipts, or total church rates.

The standard paired-page tables reproduce that structure. Column 8 records livings considered too poor to support an incumbent comfortably. Blank entries are not zero income. These observations cannot support an average-income estimate for all livings. The threshold is not necessarily uniform: Elphin explicitly treats livings below £150 annually as inadequate (p. 150).

## Particularly useful passages

- **pp. 97–98, Meath:** difficulty enforcing repayment instalments on First Fruits building loans. Persons willing to undertake collection could not be found, and bishops lacked power to compel them to accept the duty. Also discusses legal impediments to acquiring glebes, patronal consent and lay impropriators' failure to pay reserved clerical stipends.
- **pp. 128–130, Clonfert and Kilmacduagh:** explains division of parochial tithes among bishop, dignitaries and parish clergy. Forty-four of 59 parishes pay the episcopal quarta pars; allocation is not necessarily one quarter in every case. The bishop describes 59 parishes consolidated into 14 benefices. Do not add different recipients' shares to the gross tithe for the same parish.
- **p. 129:** a retrospective statement of one union's receipts for 1803–08 explicitly deducts tithe-collector and legal-process costs. This is neither current 1819 income nor gross tithe income. Figures require image-level transcription if used.
- **p. 130:** reports serious obstacles to collecting parochial cesses intended to repay First Fruits church-building loans, with arrears on the Board's books. The statement was checked against the page image.
- **pp. 139–140:** Clonfert and Kilmacduagh's bishop discusses local exclusions from tithe, contested hay tithe, tithe-proctors and possible redistribution of episcopal shares to parish incumbents. These are diocesan statements; do not generalise crop exemptions to all Ireland from this passage.
- **p. 150, Elphin:** proposes changes to the Board's authority over church repairs and the rate at which glebe-house loans were repaid. Distinguish loan repayment rates from interest rates; the passage calls them repayment rates.
- **p. 176, Dublin:** records government payments of £100 annually to the French conformist minister and £50 to the German Protestant minister. These illustrate an overlap with central expenditure, not additional independent revenue to add automatically. Currency has not been independently established.
- **p. 177 and comparable diocesan lists:** First Fruits-supported building and glebe acquisitions, dated 1801–19. These generally enumerate projects rather than provide annual expenditure totals.
- **p. 217, Kildare:** £900 granted for a proposed church at Wallerstown was to be issued from the Treasury only after legal difficulties were resolved. The project failed and the bishop moved to withdraw the allocation in February 1819. This is an authorised grant, not evidence of £900 paid. Checked against image.
- **pp. 218–219, Kildare:** family settlements restrict land transfers, complicating church and glebe provision despite parliamentary funding. The bishop proposes investing purchase money and using interest to meet rent where land cannot be bought outright. Discussion also distinguishes church-building support from ordinary repair costs and local annual assessments.
- **pp. 252–253 and 312–313:** distinguish First Fruits and twentieth parts, Primate Boulter's charitable fund, and potential parliamentary support. These are different funding streams, not a single tax yield.
- **p. 335, Leighlin and Ferns:** identifies 43 additional parishes whose tithes were enjoyed by lay impropriators. Their clerical allowances were described as insufficient. Checked against image. This is a direct warning against treating all Irish tithes as Church institutional income.
- **pp. 356–357, Ossory:** individual institutional and building problems, including an unsuitable glebe-house site, creditors' sequestration and construction liabilities. These are case observations, not annual revenue totals.

## Accounting implications

1. Distinguish compulsory tithe payments, church cess and ministers' money from rents earned on endowed property.
2. Separate ecclesiastical tithe recipients from lay impropriators when estimating Church income. Total compulsory extraction would be a different measure.
3. Do not count both benefice income and the curate's salary paid from it as separate external receipts.
4. Do not add central grants again when aggregating central, local and Church spending. Identify transfers and loans separately.
5. Separate annual income, construction expenditure, loan principal, repayment obligations and grants authorised but not disbursed.
6. Glebe acreage is not annual rental income. The return mixes explicitly Irish and English acre measures in places; preserve units and check them before aggregation.
7. No uniform currency assumption has been established for all monetary entries.

## Wider value for the book

The return shows a privileged institution whose internal distribution of resources and capacity to implement policy were uneven. Bishops report inability to collect rates, obtain land, rearrange parishes or secure resident clergy despite statutory powers and parliamentary support. This is useful evidence on the distinction between formal authority and practical capacity. These are the bishops' accounts, often written defensively in response to criticism; their explanations of failure should remain attributed.

## What is still needed

- A systematic return of tithe income, distinguishing ecclesiastical and lay ownership.
- Actual receipts from episcopal and other Church estates, including renewal fines where applicable.
- Church-cess/vestry-rate accounts. The previously identified HC 1828 (370) and HC 1828 (241) for 1827 remain leads; their contents have not been verified in this reading task.
- Ministers' money collections in the relevant towns.
- First Fruits cash accounts identifying parliamentary transfers, other receipts, grants, loans and repayments around 1822.

## Review and extraction record

All 360 scanned pages were processed with OCR. Review concentrated on the questionnaire, diocesan explanatory passages and summaries, and financial passages located across the full return. Selected key passages were checked visually. This is not a verified transcription of every table cell or a complete hand-reading of every clergy-name and property entry. OCR is especially unreliable for currency, fractions, proper names and multi-column reading order; raw text is a finding aid only.
