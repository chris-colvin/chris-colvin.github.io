02 / Who paid and what for

Who bore the cost?

1822

Central taxes

Who paid? Customs fell on importers, excise on producers and dealers in taxed goods, and stamp duties on specified documents and transactions. Consumers could bear part of these duties through prices.

What for? General government revenue supported civil administration, the armed forces, debt service and other public expenditure. It was not reserved for spending in the place where it was collected.

Grand-jury cess

Who paid? Occupiers of property, including tenant farmers. County grand juries, dominated by landowners, authorised the levy and spending.

What for? Roads and bridges, courthouses and gaols, county infirmaries and other local services.

Town and city levies

Who paid? Local householders and occupiers, under arrangements that varied by town. In Dublin, the listed local taxes were assessed using house valuations.

What for? Paving, cleansing, lighting, water, the watch, street improvements and institutions such as the Foundling Hospital.

Church tithes

Who paid? Occupiers of tithe-liable agricultural land, regardless of religion. Before the reforms beginning in 1823, tithes chiefly fell on tillage.

What for? Ecclesiastical tithes supported the established Church and its clergy. Tithes belonging to lay owners are excluded. The Church also received estate rents, which are excluded from the headline total.

The Irish Poor Law system had not yet been introduced in 1822; it began in 1838.

Sources

Central revenue and expenditure · Grand-jury cess · HC 1822 (394), p. 11, and HC 1821 (420) for Dublin · Tithes · HC 1820 (93) for Church income.

1901

Central taxes

Who paid? Consumers bore duties through purchases; income tax and death duties fell chiefly on better-off households and estates. Stamp duties applied to specified transactions.

What for? Irish services and shared UK and imperial expenditure. The accounts distinguish spending assigned to Ireland from the remaining imperial contribution.

Local rates

Who paid? Generally the occupiers of land and buildings, including farmers, householders and businesses, assessed on rateable property values. The 1898 Act retained exceptions for certain properties and tenancies.

What for? Poor relief, roads, public health, water and other municipal services, asylums and local administration. Elected councils had replaced grand juries in local government.

The Poor Law in 1901

Boards of Guardians ran workhouses, poor relief and dispensaries. Councils raised the money through property rates, generally paid by occupiers. These are included in the local-rates total.

Sources

Local Government (Ireland) Act 1898, especially ss. 52–54 · Local receipts and spending.

Source: Brassey’s fiscal tables and discussion.

National Archives: Poor Law records · 1898 Act, ss. 28, 30, 43 and 52.

Accounting notes for 1822

Central expenditure

Non-loan, non-debt expenditure: £3,665,515. Adding recorded debt interest and management of £1,115,908 gives £4,781,423.

This includes collection costs of £883,140 and other payments of £273,013 made before revenue reached the Exchequer.

Public-works advances and debt reduction are excluded. Recorded debt service does not cover every debt charge attributed to Ireland.

Coverage of the total

Dublin grand-jury cess is already included. An additional £80,000 estimates Dublin’s other taxes from 1820 collections of £94,837, less wide-street receipts (£5,800) and a precautionary quay-charge deduction (£1,813). Treating the reported pounds as Irish gives about £80,500 sterling, rounded down to £80,000. Other towns’ additional levies are excluded.

Church cess and ministers’ money are not separately estimated. Curate salaries and central grants are not added again.

04 / Explore 1901

Revenue and public services

In the financial year 1900–01, central revenue and local rates together raised £12.7m.

Central accounts

Of the £9.5m in revenue attributed to Ireland, £7.3m supported Irish services. The remaining £2.2m was recorded as the imperial contribution.

Taxes supplied most of this revenue; the Post Office, telegraphs and other non-tax receipts supplied the rest.

Local government

Local rates raised a further £3.2m, including water rates, to support local services.

Government grants transferred money from the central accounts to local authorities, so they are not counted a second time.
