The 1822 local spending estimate uses levies, and Church spending is assumed equal to tithe receipts; Church rents are excluded. The 1901 civil figure includes local capital spending. Spending excludes debt charges, and the difference is not a complete measure of transfers from Britain.

Central civil spending was £2.18m and Army and Ordnance spending £1.48m. Adding £755,000 in local levies and £500,000 in assumed Church spending gives £4.92m. Local levies stand in for expenditure and may include debt charges.

1901

£7,306,000 for Irish services plus £6,293,609 in local payments, less £885,971 in local loan charges and the central-to-local transfer overlap. The central accounts identify £1,456,000 in local-account payments, while local authorities report £1,296,039 in government contributions. The two deductions give civil spending of £11.26m–£11.42m. Army spending is estimated at 21,235 troops × £110 a year = £2,335,850, assumed entirely spent in Ireland. No separate Navy or pension estimate is added.

Loan charges are excluded in full because principal and interest are combined. Local gas and electricity accounts report net profits rather than gross spending.

02 / Who paid and what for

Who bore the cost?

1822

Central taxes

Who paid? Customs fell on importers, excise on producers and dealers in taxed goods, and stamp duties on specified documents and transactions. Consumers could bear part of these duties through prices.

Ireland still had distinct customs arrangements, including duties on some trade with Great Britain. Duties were set by the UK Parliament; the Exchequers had merged in 1817, before the customs changes of 1823–26.

What for? General government revenue supported civil administration, the armed forces, debt service and other public expenditure. It was not reserved for spending in the place where it was collected.

Local levies

Grand-jury cess

Who paid? Occupiers of property, including tenant farmers. County grand juries, dominated by landowners, authorised the levy and spending.

What for? Roads and bridges, courthouses and gaols, county infirmaries and other local services.

Town and city levies

Who paid? Local householders and occupiers, under arrangements that varied by town. In Dublin, these taxes were assessed using house valuations.

What for? Paving, cleansing, lighting, water, the watch, street improvements and institutions such as the Foundling Hospital.

Church tithes

Who paid? Occupiers of tithe-liable agricultural land, regardless of religion. Before the reforms beginning in 1823, tithes chiefly fell on tillage.

What for? Tithes supported the established Church and its clergy. The totals exclude tithes paid to lay owners and Church estate rents.

The Irish Poor Law system had not yet been introduced in 1822; it began in 1838.

Sources

Central revenue and expenditure · Grand-jury cess · HC 1822 (394), p. 11, and HC 1821 (420) for Dublin · Tithes · HC 1820 (93) for Church income.

Customs arrangements: 1823 Act on duties between Great Britain and Ireland · 1823 Act equalising specified external customs duties · Murray, chapter 17, on the 1817 Exchequer merger and 1826 customs changes.

1901

Central taxes

Who paid? Customs and excise fell on importers, producers and dealers in taxed goods, including tea, tobacco and alcoholic drinks. Consumers bore part of the cost through prices, so these taxes reached households well below the income-tax threshold.

Income tax fell on taxable incomes from property, business and employment; death duties on estates at death. These chiefly affected better-off households. Stamp duties applied to specified legal documents and transactions.

What for? General UK revenue supported services in Ireland, including education, police, courts, prisons and civil administration, alongside shared expenditure on the armed forces and national debt. Taxes collected in Ireland were not reserved for Irish spending.

Local levies

Who paid? Generally the occupiers of land and buildings, including farmers, householders and businesses, assessed on rateable property values. The 1898 Act retained exceptions for certain properties and tenancies.

What for? County rates supported roads, asylums and county administration, as well as poor relief. Town and city rates funded services such as water, sanitation and street maintenance. Elected county and district councils had taken over the grand juries’ local-government responsibilities.

The Poor Law in 1901

Boards of Guardians ran workhouses, poor relief and dispensaries, funded through property rates generally paid by occupiers.

Church disestablishment

The Church of Ireland ceased to be the established Church in 1871, under the Irish Church Act 1869. By 1901 it governed and financed itself independently of the state. Its income and spending are therefore excluded from the fiscal totals here.

Sources

Local Government (Ireland) Act 1898, especially ss. 52–54 · Local receipts and spending.

Brassey’s fiscal tables and discussion.

National Archives: Poor Law records · 1898 Act, ss. 28, 30, 43 and 52.

Church disestablishment: Irish Church Act 1869, section 2 · Church of Ireland historical account.

CENTRAL / FEDERAL REVENUE

Ireland: 1822 uses central ordinary revenue and national output from the 1822 account. For 1901, central taxes come from Brassey, appendix table I, divided by the output estimate in the sources below.

UK in 1822: Bank of England, A millennium of macroeconomic data, v3.1. Central revenue of £59.9m divided by UK output of £453.0m at market prices gives 13.2%. Both cover Great Britain and Ireland.

Great Britain in 1901: Brassey, appendix table I, revenue for 1900–01, divided by the Bank of England output estimate. £110.6m in taxes against £1,723.8m in output. These taxes include centrally collected revenue assigned to local taxation accounts, but exclude local property rates.

France: Andersson and Brambor, Financing the State, v3.0, central tax revenue as a percentage of GDP.

United States: Andersson and Brambor, Financing the State, v3.0, for revenue and GDP. Federal receipts include non-tax revenue, as confirmed by US Historical Statistics, series P 89–98.

National output estimates come from different reconstructions, so the comparisons are approximate.

STATE / LOCAL REVENUE

Ireland: £755,000 in grand-jury and additional Dublin levies plus £500,000 in estimated tithes in 1822 (£1,255,000 total), and £3,642,792 in local rates, tolls and fees in 1900–01, divided by the output figures used above.

Great Britain, 1822: £7,761,441 in England and Wales poor rates, plus £49,719 in Scottish assessments, gives £7.81m, or 2.0% of GB output (£385.17m; Bank of England). Scotland uses the 1807–16 annual average reported in 1820 as a proxy: Nicholls, History of the Scotch Poor Law, chapter II.

Great Britain, 1900–01: UK rates of £50,648,788 less Irish rates of £3,173,621 = £47,475,167, divided by £1,723.8m in output. Statistical Abstract, table 21, p. 52. Grants and borrowing excluded.

France, 1822 comparison: net octrois of 44.67m francs (1823), plus departmental surtaxes of 9.66m, communal surtaxes of 18.42m and cadastral surtaxes of 3.61m (1826), give 76.36m francs, or 0.8% of 1822 output (9,045m francs; Andersson and Brambor v3.0). Sources: Legoyt, Les octrois en France, pp. 175–176, and Gazette des tribunaux, 22 April 1829, p. 3, reporting the 1826 accounts. This is a partial estimate using nearby years. Octrois are net of collection costs and deductions, including the contribution mobilière. Some departmental taxes also appear in central accounts, so the central and local figures should not simply be added.

France, 1900: departmental and communal taxes of 721m francs, from André and Delorme, 1973 study, table II-1, divided by 32,806m francs in nominal output from Andersson and Brambor v3.0. Non-tax receipts and transfers excluded.

United States: Wallis (2000), table 1. The 1820 state-only estimate gives 0.8% of GDP using census population and Andersson–Brambor output. For 1902, state (0.8%) and local (4.0%) revenue total 4.8% of GNP. Both include non-tax receipts.

Central expenditure

Non-loan, non-debt expenditure: £3,665,515. Adding recorded debt interest and management of £1,115,908 gives £4,781,423.

This includes collection costs of £883,140 and other payments of £273,013 made before revenue reached the Exchequer.

Public-works advances and debt reduction are excluded. Recorded debt service does not cover every debt charge attributed to Ireland.

Coverage of the total

The £80,000 Dublin estimate uses 1820 collections, adjusted for currency and possible overlap with grand-jury cess. Additional levies outside Dublin are excluded.

Church cess and ministers’ money are not separately estimated. Curate salaries and central grants are not added again.

Civil spending combines central payments excluding debt, loans and military spending with the estimated local levies.

Tithe-funded Church expenditure

Church spending is estimated at the same amount as tithe receipts: £500,000, with £400,000 and £650,000 alternatives. This is an estimate, not a recorded spending total. The benchmark is £534,433 in tithe composition reported by Lord John Russell, 30 March 1835, cols. 368–369. Church rents and other unquantified Church levies are excluded.

Grand-jury currency conversion

Antrim, Drogheda, Limerick county and Limerick city reported Irish pounds, converted to sterling at 12/13. Sligo’s figures are already in sterling and are left unchanged. Cork city and Louth probably used Irish pounds; the other returns are unclear. Different treatments of these uncertain entries give the £650,000–700,000 range. Tyrone is excluded because data are unavailable.
